## 1. The Problem

America is attempting the largest infrastructure build-out since the interstate highway system, and it is failing at the last mile — not for lack of capital, chips, or power engineering, but at a single, badly handled moment: the first negotiation between a project and the community asked to host it.

Every data center project has one moment when the operator and the community sit at the same table with everything still open. It happens exactly once — **there is no second first negotiation.** Handled deliberately, with genuine transparency, it can found a working relationship durable enough to carry both parties through decades of change. Handled as a permitting gate — rushed, adversarial, disclosed to the minimum — it produces, at best, a permit and a grievance.

Today it is handled as a gate, and increasingly it produces neither. **In the first quarter of 2026 alone, communities blocked or delayed more than 75 data center projects worth roughly $130 billion — matching the disruption of all of 2025 in three months.** The trend is not cyclical; it is structural and accelerating. Active local opposition groups more than doubled in a single quarter, from 396 to 833, and now operate in 49 states. Moratorium bills were introduced in 14 state legislatures in Q1 2026, plus a federal version. As of mid-2026, more than 100 municipalities have enacted local moratoriums. Gallup finds 71% of Americans oppose a data center near them — 48% strongly. The opposition is bipartisan, organized, and learning from itself: communities have internalized a playbook.

Communities are not wrong. They are being asked to absorb industrial-scale water draw, noise, land conversion, and — through wholesale power markets — real increases in their electric bills, in exchange for tax revenue that arrives later and jobs that are fewer than promised. The current model asks them to take diffuse, permanent costs in exchange for concentrated, uncertain benefits, and gives them no instrument to price the difference or negotiate for it. When people lack a fair mechanism, they use the one they have: **no**.

And the permit was the wrong prize anyway. A data center shell is engineered for twenty-five years or more; the substation, water infrastructure, and tax agreements around it run decades. The hardware inside is on a different clock entirely — a three-to-five-year refresh cycle, a new chip architecture every eighteen months, and a live Wall Street fight over whether an AI GPU is really useful past three. Over its life, one building will host eight or ten generations of hardware, each changing its demands on the grid, the water system, and the neighbors — from workload mix to cooling technology to the shape of the campus itself, in ways neither party can specify at signing or even on the first day of operations. No rezoning approval covers that. Only a working relationship does — built on trust, in a moment of real transparency, around a shared and credible measure of the value each side brings. **Build the relationship, not just the project.** For the industry this is not sentiment; it is necessity: community standing is now financially material at $10-billion-per-campus scale, regulation is near-certain (operators who can *demonstrate* community value will shape those rules and comply cheaply), and a durable social license is the only instrument that survives the changes every project will undergo.

What is missing is not goodwill — it is equipment. The negotiation fails because no shared measure of community value exists, so each side argues from anecdote; because every available number is somebody's advocacy, so none is trusted; because the rezoning calendar forces speed exactly where deliberateness pays; and because nothing durable exists for the parties to return to when the project changes. Nobody's job is to build that equipment. Developers optimize single deals. Consultants sell to one side. Advocacy groups organize the other. What is needed is a trusted, independent institution that can define community value, measure it, price it into real transactions, and publish the evidence — the role a university is built for.

## 2. The Delay-Cost Arithmetic

Philanthropy for this initiative is not charity; it is the highest-leverage investment available in the AI build-out, and the arithmetic is not close.

A single hyperscale campus represents $5–15 billion in capital expenditure. A one-year delay on one $10 billion project — carrying cost alone, at prevailing cost of capital, before counting foregone compute revenue — destroys on the order of **$700 million to $1 billion in value**. A blocked project destroys the whole thing. Q1 2026's toll was ~$130 billion across 75+ projects.

Against that: this initiative operates at full scale for roughly **[$3–5M] per year**. If the tools, evidence, and standards it produces convert *one* contested project per decade from blocked to approved-with-community-value — or shave *one year* off *one* campus's approval timeline — the initiative returns its entire multi-decade cost more than a hundred times over. This is the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety model: a modestly funded, rigorously independent research institution whose evidence quietly restructures how an entire industry prices risk. IIHS crash ratings changed automotive design without a single regulation. Community-value evidence can change siting the same way — because the money already wants to move and is being stopped by an information problem. And this model is not borrowed on faith: the initiative's founder has already run it once, at national scale, with RWJF's backing (§4).

## 3. Why a University — and Why UVA

The core asset in this market is trust, and trust is exactly what no existing actor can supply. Developer-funded studies are discounted by communities; advocacy research is discounted by capital. A university — with peer review, independence, and no position in any deal — is the only institution whose numbers both sides can accept. This is why GRESB (real estate ESG benchmarking, born academic) and IIHS became load-bearing infrastructure for their industries: the referee role cannot be bought, only earned.

UVA is not merely a plausible home; it is arguably the single best-positioned university in the country:

- **Geography.** Virginia is the data center capital of the world, and Virginia is where the conflict is sharpest — Warrenton voters removed every council member who supported a proposed data center. The natural laboratory is in the Commonwealth, an hour from Grounds.
- **Darden and the Center for Real Estate.** The problem is, at bottom, a real-estate-finance and strategy problem: how to underwrite community value in a pro forma. Darden faculty are already publishing on data centers' community costs; the Center for Real Estate provides the practitioner network and convening platform.
- **The LaCross AI Institute.** UVA has already committed institutionally to leadership on AI and business; this initiative is the *infrastructure and society* pillar that commitment implies, and Darden faculty affiliated with that work are natural collaborators.
- **Cross-Grounds depth.** Environmental economics, biocomplexity and large-scale simulation, landscape architecture and land use — UVA holds every discipline this problem touches, with allies already in place (see §6).
- **UVA has done this before.** The Green Health Partnership — the decade-long, RWJF-funded market-transformation partnership that put health metrics into LEED and GRESB (§4) — was formally a UVA School of Medicine initiative. For the University, this is not an experiment in a new model; it is a repeat performance of one UVA has already run at national scale.

## 4. The Founder: A Proven Playbook

The strongest evidence that this initiative will work is not analogy; it is demonstration.

Matt Trowbridge, MD, MPH — physician, public health researcher, and Associate Professor at the UVA School of Medicine — is internationally recognized for translational research on how built environments shape health, well-being, and social value. His career has been spent making those values *measurable and investable*. As Principal Investigator of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation–funded Green Health Partnership between UVA and the U.S. Green Building Council, he took a diffuse, unpriced value — health — and installed it inside the instruments real-estate capital obeys: a LEED pilot credit, modeled explicitly on health impact assessment, used by 200+ projects across LEED, Enterprise Green Communities, and Australia's Green Star; the first portfolio-level health metrics in GRESB's ESG benchmark, used by 252 portfolios covering 32,000+ assets; and AIA continuing-education modules reaching 90,000+ architects. As Chief Medical Officer of the International WELL Building Institute he then directed health strategy for a certification system spanning tens of thousands of projects worldwide. He serves on the Technical Working Group for the 2026 Enterprise Green Communities Criteria, advises ARPA-H on indoor air quality technologies, and today works directly with data center developers on health impact assessment inside live projects.

He stated this initiative's thesis publicly a decade ago, at Greenbuild 2015: the community impacts of real estate development "operate largely as economic externalities: unmeasured, unregulated, and for the most part unconsidered… healthy places must also become investable."

Together with the IIHS precedent (§2), the proof structure is complete: IIHS shows that a modestly funded, independent research institution can restructure how an industry prices risk; the Green Health Partnership shows that this founder, at this university, can run that model for the built environment. This initiative is not a pivot — it is the second application of a proven, decade-long research program, aimed at the defining land-use conflict of the AI era.

## 5. The Initiative: Three Arms

If the aspiration is *build the relationship, not just the project*, the initiative's job is to make that possible in practice — by building the equipment the negotiation currently lacks. Its method is the transformation the founder has led once before: making community value **measurable and investable**. In one sentence, the initiative exists to **equip the negotiation, sustain the relationship, and scale the practice** — one arm for each:

**Arm 1 — Equip the negotiation: practitioner tools (private until validated).** The wedge asset is a community-value pricing tool that translates community costs and benefits into the language developers actually use: the pro forma. The tool is developed and iterated *privately, under NDA, with operators* — not published — until it has been validated in real transactions with operators and community stakeholders and deepened with technical content on key topics (power, water, noise, fiscal impact). Premature publication would invite attack before the instrument is defensible; validated deployment inside live deals is what makes it real. This is deliberately the opposite of the think-tank model: credibility with practitioners first, visibility second.

**Arm 2 — Sustain the relationship: open scholarship (the dataset and the papers).** A relationship that must absorb decades of change needs a shared ledger both parties trust — evidence they can return to each time the project evolves. In parallel with the tools work, the initiative builds a new research dataset on data center siting outcomes, community terms, and opposition dynamics — the collaboration platform with Mike Lenox, in the tradition of his industry-transformation datasets. The dataset feeds peer-reviewed publication and, deliberately, high-impact popular writing: op-eds and commentary that establish UVA — and the initiative's faculty — as *the* academic voice on data centers and community value. The scholarship arm is public where the tools arm is private; each protects the other. The dataset also becomes the recruiting mechanism for additional faculty: economists and strategy scholars join where the data is.

**Arm 3 — Scale the practice: standards partnerships.** The path from one-off deals to industry transformation runs through standards. The initiative will pilot versions of its validated instruments with high-profile standards bodies — **LEED, GRESB, and peers** — so that community value becomes something the industry benchmarks, reports, and eventually competes on. This follows the proven sequence: benchmark before you certify. GRESB began as an academic benchmarking survey and became the instrument institutional capital requires; a community-value benchmark, piloted through existing standards infrastructure rather than built from scratch, is the fastest route to the same position. Critically, this is not a cold start: Arm 3 runs on relationships and a playbook the founder already owns (§4). It begins in year two, once Arm 1 has produced instruments worth piloting.

**How the work runs.** In practice the three arms are delivered through five workstreams: **industry engagement and learning** (practitioner interviews, NDA engagements inside live projects), **convening** (bringing operators, communities, officials, and scholars to the same table), **research** (the dataset, peer-reviewed papers, public writing), **tool development**, and **standards development**. Every stream operates under the same published operating principles: transparency about methods and funding, rigor that survives peer review, independence from any position in any deal, and service to both sides of the table — the negotiation is treated as the deliberate, intentional process it deserves to be, never a gate to be rushed.

**Who benefits.** The test of the aspiration is that every stakeholder does better inside the relationship than outside it. **Host communities** get a credible seat and a real ledger — costs and benefits priced, not asserted — and leverage that doesn't require a moratorium. **Community advocates** get independent, rigorous data in place of anecdote, and a constructive channel that doesn't demand "no." **The AI infrastructure industry** — operators, developers, and the hyperscalers behind them — gets faster and more durable approvals, a social license that survives hardware and workload change, and de-risked capital. **Local and state officials** get a neutral evidence base for conditions and agreements instead of one-off fights. **Those responsible for economic growth and U.S. competitiveness** — from county economic development to federal policy — get a build-out that proceeds without steamrolling the communities it lands in. **Capital and the broader real estate industry** get community risk they can finally price and underwrite. **Standards bodies** get validated content for their next material topic, and **the research community** gets a new dataset and a new field.

## 7. Year One Scoreboard

Twelve months from funding, success is measured on two axes — operator depth and scholarly voice — because the first proves the instrument and the second establishes the institution:

**Operator depth:**
- Pricing tool iterated through structured engagements with **3+ operators under NDA**, including at least one use inside a live pro forma.
- **~50 practitioner interviews** completed across developers, utilities, local officials, and community leaders.
- **Dataset v1** built with Lenox and in active analysis.
- **One UVA convening** bringing operators, communities, and scholars to the same table.

**Scholarly voice:**
- **1–2 peer-reviewed submissions** from the dataset and interview corpus.
- **High-impact popular placements** — op-eds and commentary in national outlets — establishing the founder and UVA as the reference academic voice on this topic.

## 8. The Funding Ladder

- **Rung 1 — Philanthropic seed (this ask): [$X over 3 years].** Buys the founder's protected time, a research director/analyst, the operator engagements, dataset construction, and the first convening. Philanthropy is structurally necessary at this stage precisely because the initiative's value *is* its independence — it cannot be seeded by the industry it will referee.
- **Rung 2 — Industry participation (years 2–4).** Once instruments are validated, operators and capital providers pay to participate — benchmark membership, data partnerships, standards pilots — on the GRESB model, where industry funds the referee because the referee's number is what their investors demand.
- **Rung 3 — Institutional permanence.** A named center and endowment conversation, once the initiative has proven it belongs among UVA's defining commitments.

## 9. The Ask

**[$X million over three years]** to launch the Community Value Initiative — and, more than money, a partnership: introductions into the donor's networks in [industry/capital/policy], counsel on the standards strategy, and a founding stake in building the institution that resolves the defining land-use conflict of the AI era.

The window is now. The opposition playbook is compounding quarter over quarter; the industry is reaching for preemption, which will deepen the conflict rather than resolve it; and no university has yet claimed the referee's chair. In eighteen months one will have. It should be Virginia's.
